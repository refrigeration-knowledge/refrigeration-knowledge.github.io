Refrigeration Compressors: Types, Mechanics and Compression Ratios

The compressor is the heart of the vapour-compression refrigeration cycle. It functions as both a vapour pump to circulate refrigerant mass flow and a pressure-elevation machine that bridges the gap between low evaporating temperatures and high condensing temperatures.

Primary Compressor Mechanical Architectures

Compressor Type Mechanical Principle Typical Applications Key Advantages
Reciprocating (Piston) Crankshaft drives pistons in cylinders with reed suction and discharge valves Domestic refrigerators, small coolrooms, commercial freezers High pressure ratios, proven reliability, forgiving of oil return variations
Rotary (Rolling Piston) Eccentric roller on a shaft rotating within a cylinder with sliding vane Domestic split systems, portable fridges, small reach-in units Compact physical footprint, minimal parts, quiet steady operation
Scroll (Orbital Spiral) One fixed involute spiral scroll mesh with one orbiting scroll Commercial coolrooms, supermarket packs, large heat pumps Zero clearance volume loss, 100% volumetric efficiency, smooth discharge
Screw (Helical Rotor) Male and female intermeshing helical rotors compressing gas axially Industrial cold stores, chilled water plants (>100 kW) Continuous slide-valve capacity control (10% to 100%), extreme lifespan

Compression Ratio and Volumetric Efficiency

Compression Ratio ($R_c$): The ratio of absolute discharge pressure to absolute suction pressure:
R_c = P_discharge_(absolute) / P_suction_(absolute)
Note: Always calculate using absolute pressure (kPa gauge + 101.3 kPa).

As the compression ratio increases (for example, in a low-temperature freezer operating at -25°C evaporating against a 40°C Australian summer condensing condition), volumetric efficiency declines rapidly.

In reciprocating compressors, high compression ratios cause high-pressure vapour trapped in the cylinder clearance volume to re-expand during the downward piston stroke, preventing fresh suction vapour from entering until the cylinder pressure drops below suction line pressure. When $R_c$ exceeds 9:1, two-stage compression or economised scroll circuits become necessary to prevent oil carbonisation.

Refrigeration Oil Circulation & Miscibility

Refrigeration compressors require constant lubrication of bearings, wrist pins, and scroll flanks. Unlike an automobile engine, a refrigeration compressor continuously discharges a fine mist of lubricating oil into the refrigerant stream:

  • Refrigerant-Oil Miscibility: The oil (such as Polyolester / POE for HFCs/HFOs, or Polyvinyl Ether / PVE for R32/R410A) must remain miscible with liquid refrigerant at low evaporator temperatures so that oil washed into the coil can flow through suction risers back to the crankcase.
  • Minimum Suction Velocity: Horizontal suction lines require at least 2.5 m/s refrigerant gas velocity, and vertical risers require 5.0 to 6.0 m/s with oil traps to lift oil against gravity.

Inverter (BLDC) Modulation vs Fixed-Speed Cycles

Modern refrigeration compressors increasingly employ Brushless DC (BLDC) interior permanent magnet motors driven by Pulse Width Modulation (PWM) variable-frequency inverters.

Instead of brutal start-stop cycling that stresses mechanical bearings and causes temperature swings, inverter compressors dynamically modulate motor speed between 1,200 RPM and 4,500 RPM to precisely match thermal load. Learn how this drastically boosts system Refrigeration Energy Efficiency.

Frequently Asked Questions

What causes a compressor to 'slug'?

Slugging occurs when liquid refrigerant or massive slugs of liquid oil enter the compressor suction port. Because liquids are incompressible, the piston attempt to compress liquid generates extreme hydraulic pressures that shatter valve plates, bend connecting rods, and snap crankshafts.

Why does an inverter compressor run almost 24 hours a day?

Continuous operation at low speed (20Hz–30Hz) is an intentional design feature. Running continuously at low displacement consumes far less electrical energy than stopping and starting at 50Hz, while eliminating thermal cycling in the refrigerated space.

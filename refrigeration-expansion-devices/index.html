Refrigeration Expansion Devices: TXV, EEV and Capillary Tube Engineering

The expansion device marks the critical boundary separating the high-pressure liquid condensing side from the low-pressure evaporating side. It performs two simultaneous thermodynamic functions: dropping refrigerant pressure to lower its boiling saturation point and modulating the exact mass flow rate into the evaporator to match active thermal load.

Thermodynamics of Isenthalpic Expansion (Throttling)

When liquid refrigerant passes through a narrow restriction orifice without performing external shaft work ($W=0$) and with negligible heat exchange ($Q=0$), the process is adiabatic and isenthalpic ($h_1 = h_2$).

Flash Gas Generation: Because the downstream pressure is drastically lower, the saturation temperature drops below the liquid's current temperature. The excess sensible heat instantly causes a fraction of the liquid to flash into gas:
Flash Gas Fraction (x) = (h_inlet - h_f_evap) / h_fg_evap
Typically, 15% to 25% of the liquid flashes into vapour. The latent heat extracted to produce this flash vapour chills the remaining 75% to 85% of liquid to the low evaporating temperature.

Comparison of Expansion Technologies

Device Category Operating Principle Superheat Modulation Typical Applications
Capillary Tube Fixed copper micro-bore tube (0.6mm to 2.0mm ID) providing frictional pressure drop None (fixed resistance; self-balancing only over narrow load range) Domestic refrigerators, small freezers, room air conditioners
Thermostatic Expansion Valve (TXV/TEV) Mechanical diaphragm balancing sensing bulb pressure against spring and evaporator pressure Dynamic mechanical modulation (maintains constant 4K–8K superheat) Commercial coolrooms, reach-in commercial cabinets, chilled display cases
Electronic Expansion Valve (EEV) Direct-drive bipolar stepper motor positioning a micro-needle orifice (typically 500–2000 steps) Precision digital PID algorithm using pressure transducer & suction thermistor Inverter heat pumps, variable refrigerant flow (VRF), modern supermarket racks

Mechanics of the TXV: The Three Operating Forces

A mechanical thermostatic expansion valve positions its internal needle via a flexible stainless steel diaphragm subjected to three continuous forces:

P1 (Opening) = P2 (Closing) + P3 (Closing)
  • $P_1$ (Opening Force): Vapour pressure generated inside the thermal sensing bulb clamped to the evaporator outlet. As superheat rises (indicating warm suction gas), $P_1$ increases, pushing the diaphragm downward to open the orifice.
  • $P_2$ (Closing Force): Evaporator refrigerant pressure acting on the underside of the diaphragm.
  • $P_3$ (Closing Force): Mechanical superheat spring pressure adjusted manually by the technician via the bottom stem.

Internal vs External Pressure Equalisation

In small single-circuit evaporators where refrigerant pressure drop across the coil is under 15 kPa, an internally equalised TXV measures evaporator pressure directly at the valve outlet.

In large commercial evaporators featuring distributor tubes and multiple circuit passes, friction creates a substantial pressure drop (often 30 kPa to 50 kPa) across the coil. An internal equalisation port would measure an artificially high pressure, causing the valve to starve the evaporator. An externally equalised TXV features an external 1/4" copper pilot line connected to the suction line downstream of the sensing bulb, ensuring accurate superheat calculation.

The EEV Advantage: Precision & Floating Head Pressure

Unlike mechanical TXVs which require at least 400 kPa pressure differential to operate predictably, Electronic Expansion Valves (EEVs) operate accurately even under low condensing head pressures during cool weather. This allows systems to run with "floating head pressure", drastically reducing compressor power consumption and increasing system energy efficiency.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is 'valve hunting'?

Hunting occurs when a TXV continuously overfeeds and underfeeds the evaporator in a cyclic wave. The valve opens wide, flooding liquid into the coil; the sensing bulb gets cold and snaps the valve shut, starving the coil until superheat spikes again. Hunting is caused by oversized valve orifices, incorrect bulb placement, or poor thermal contact.

Where should the TXV sensing bulb be mounted?

The bulb must be clamped securely to a horizontal run of clean copper suction pipe immediately after the evaporator outlet. For suction lines smaller than 7/8", mount at 1 o'clock or 2 o'clock. For larger lines (>7/8"), mount at 4 o'clock or 8 o'clock. Never mount a bulb at the bottom (6 o'clock), where accumulated oil acts as an insulator and gives false temperature readings.

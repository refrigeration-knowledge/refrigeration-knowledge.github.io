Condenser vs Evaporator: Heat Exchanger Mechanics and Thermodynamics

Both condensers and evaporators are heat exchangers designed to transfer thermal energy between a circulating refrigerant fluid and a secondary medium (air, water, or brine). However, their thermodynamic objectives, phase change directions, pressure regimes, and fin surface geometries are entirely mirror-inverted.

Direct Thermodynamic Comparison

Parameter Condenser (Heat Rejection) Evaporator (Heat Absorption)
Thermodynamic Role Rejects total heat ($Q_{out} = Q_{evap} + W_{comp}$) Extracts heat from conditioned space ($Q_{in}$)
Refrigerant Phase Change Superheated Vapour → Saturated Liquid Subcooled Liquid/Flash Gas → Superheated Vapour
Operating Pressure High Side (Discharge Pressure) Low Side (Suction Pressure)
Internal Thermal Regimes 1. De-superheating → 2. Condensing → 3. Subcooling 1. Flash gas expansion → 2. Boiling → 3. Superheating
Typical Fin Pitch (Air-Cooled) Tight: 14 to 22 fins per inch (FPI) Wide: 4 to 8 FPI (freezers) or 10 to 14 FPI (chilled)
Condensation Occurrence Internal (refrigerant condenses inside tubes) External (atmospheric moisture condenses on airside fins)

Log Mean Temperature Difference (LMTD)

The rate of thermal heat transfer ($Q$) across both coils is governed by the Fourier-Newton heat exchange equation:

Q = U × A × LMTD

Where $U$ is the overall heat transfer coefficient ($W/m^2 \cdot K$), $A$ is the total finned surface area ($m^2$), and $LMTD$ is the Log Mean Temperature Difference between refrigerant saturation temperature and air inlet/outlet temperatures.

Impact of Coil Fouling: When dirt, pet hair, or kitchen grease deposits on condenser fins, the conductive heat transfer coefficient $U$ drops by over 50%. To compensate and reject the required $Q_{out}$, the system must automatically raise the condensing temperature ($T_{cond}$), elevating head pressure and straining the compressor motor.

Why Evaporator Fin Spacing is Wider than Condenser Spacing

Appliance designers intentionally manufacture evaporators with much wider fin spacing (lower fins per inch) than condensers:

  • Frost Accumulation: Operating below 0°C, atmospheric moisture extracted from room air freezes directly onto the aluminum fins as frost. If an evaporator had tight 20-FPI spacing like a condenser, frost would bridge the fin gaps within 2 hours, choking all airflow. Freezers use wide 4 to 6 FPI spacing so air channels remain open between defrost intervals.
  • Pressure Drop: Wide fin spacing minimises airside static pressure drop, allowing compact, low-noise shaded-pole or ECM blower fans to move adequate CFM across the coil.

Refrigerant Circuiting & Distribution

In multi-circuit commercial evaporators, refrigerant exiting the expansion valve enters a venturi brass distributor with equal-length capillary feeder lines. This ensures every individual tube circuit receives an identical mixture of liquid and flash gas, preventing "short-circuiting" where one pass starves while another floods.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does water run off an evaporator coil but not a condenser coil?

Air contains moisture. When warm air cools below its dewpoint temperature against cold evaporator fins, water vapour condenses out of the air onto the metal exterior as liquid condensate. In condensers, the metal is hotter than ambient air, so no external condensation occurs.

What happens if an evaporator fan stops spinning?

Heat transfer plummets ($Q_{in} \approx 0$). Without ambient heat to boil the liquid refrigerant, liquid fails to vaporise in the coil and flows down the suction line directly into the compressor, causing catastrophic mechanical liquid slugging.
